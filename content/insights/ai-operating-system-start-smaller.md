Start with what your firm already knows, and with where that knowledge lives right now. McKinsey's 2026 State of AI survey found that 40 percent of respondents at large companies say they have scaled AI agents, but only 6 percent say AI adds even 5 percent to EBIT ([McKinsey, October 2026](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-ai-operating-system-a-new-way-for-ceos-to-run-the-enterprise)). McKinsey's prescription is what it calls an AI operating system: a shared layer that holds what a company knows, reasons across teams, and recommends or makes decisions. The rule it attaches is the sharpest part of the piece: "own the context, rent or replace the technology" ([McKinsey, October 2026](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-ai-operating-system-a-new-way-for-ceos-to-run-the-enterprise)). Most firms have that backwards. They rent the technology, which is fine, and they rent the context too, which is not. It sits in personal chat accounts and in the heads of people who will eventually leave. The place to start is not the platform decision. It is the context.

## Why do scaled agents fail to move earnings?

The distance between 40 percent and 6 percent is a context gap ([McKinsey, October 2026](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/quantumblack/our-insights/the-ai-operating-system-a-new-way-for-ceos-to-run-the-enterprise)). An agent that does not know how the firm reads a deal, what it flagged last quarter, what it decided and why, produces output that someone senior has to redo. The agent ran. The work did not get done. Multiply that across a company and you get the survey result: lots of scaling, very little earnings.

The firm-wide operating system McKinsey describes stalls for a related reason. Drawn at company scale, it reads as a steering committee and a year of integration work before anything useful appears, the kind of project that quietly dies in the second quarter. The part that makes it worth running is not the integration. It is the written context underneath, and that part is also the only part that survives when the technology changes.

## What walks out the door, and what stays?

The context McKinsey says to own is exactly what leaves when someone resigns. A year of prompts and corrections inside a personal account. The reasoning behind a decision that lives in one person's memory. The way a particular analyst learned to spot the problem file, which nobody wrote down because nobody had to. On the Monday after notice, all of it is gone, and the next hire rebuilds it one conversation at a time.

Owning the context means writing those things down where the firm holds them, not the individual. The firm's standards. Its precedents. Its decisions, with the reasons attached. Who its people are and what is open with each of them. None of that requires a platform. It requires someone to decide that the firm's knowledge belongs to the firm, and then to keep the files current.

## What does owning the context look like in practice?

One useful way to break it down is four pieces, the same shape McKinsey describes for the enterprise.

**What it knows.** The firm's standards, precedents, open commitments, and the way it likes decisions framed. Written down in plain files the firm controls, not generic advice.

**What it can reach.** The calendar, the inbox, the documents, the systems of record. It sees what is coming and what is slipping before anyone asks.

**What it can do.** Draft the memo. Prep the meeting. Chase the missing document. Summarize the thread. With a person reviewing what it produces.

**How often it runs.** On its own, on a schedule, so the morning brief is waiting before the day starts.

Built on plain files the firm controls, the context is not locked inside anyone's platform. Change the underlying model tomorrow and lose nothing. The context is the asset. The technology is rented. That is McKinsey's rule, and it holds at any scale.

## Where should one person start?

The fastest way to believe any of this is to run it for yourself for a week.

Forget the vision deck. Make a folder of plain text files: one for your goals, one for your people, one for your open commitments, one for how you like to work. Each one short, updated as things change. Then put an agentic AI tool in front of them that reads those files before it answers anything, and reads them again before it runs on a schedule.

Get the files right before automating anything. A morning brief built on good context is useful on day one. A morning brief built on nothing is just noise.

Once you have run your own day this way, the company version stops being abstract. You stop asking what agentic AI can do for the firm and start asking why the firm's context is still sitting in people's heads.

## Which side of this are you on?

Most executives fall into one of two groups. One has tried agentic AI tools and found them shallow, because the tools knew nothing about the actual work. The other has not tried at all, because nobody has shown them what it looks like when the tool knows the business.

Both are asking the same question, whether or not they say it out loud: is my firm building something it will keep, or watching a competitor build it first?

McKinsey's answer is to build the operating system. We agree. We just think the first thing you build is the context, and the first place you prove it is your own desk.
