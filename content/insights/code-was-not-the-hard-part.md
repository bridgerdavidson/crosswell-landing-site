Not the code. One owner, not an engineer by trade, built his financial services firm its own agentic AI system, agentic AI meaning AI that does the work rather than just answering questions, over the past year at his own desk, tool by tool, failure by failure, until his team ran on it. The hard part turned out to be something else entirely: collecting what his firm actually knows and feeding it into the system. His answer to where the value lives came in three parts, none of them the part the demos show: the tool built for the firm's actual work, the memory that keeps what the firm learns, and software people use because it fits them.

## Why isn't the interface the value?

His sharpest warning came unprompted. The AI platforms are making their tools easier every month. What took a terminal and a manual at the start of the year is a phone app now. So a friendlier window on a model is a melting asset. In his words: the value is not putting a skin on AI. It is building the actual tool people need.

The market is moving in the same direction, on a narrower claim than his. Over the past few months of 2026, the largest AI and cloud companies have committed or attracted roughly $9 billion to ventures that embed engineers inside customer organizations to build working systems ([VC Cafe, August 2026](https://www.vccafe.com/the-9-billion-bet-on-forward-deployed-engineers/); AWS's $1 billion commitment is [documented directly](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/aws/aws-1-billion-forward-deployed-ai-engineers)), and by mid-2026, 70 percent of surveyed companies planned to hire forward-deployed engineers, up from under 10 percent at the start of the year ([TechCrunch, July 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/30/forward-deployed-engineers-are-the-ai-industrys-latest-talent-obsession/)). What that proves: implementation, not model access, is the scarce skill. Whether the resulting systems should be firm-owned, memory-first, and personal to each user is his thesis, not the market's. The rest of this piece is his case for it.

## Why does rented software never feel like yours?

His starting frustration will sound familiar to anyone who signs the invoices. You pay for features you never touch, and the small features that would genuinely change your week never get built, because your firm is not worth the vendor's roadmap. Request, ticket, "we will take that under advisement," nothing.

The gap between what firms buy and what they use is measurable: the average organization uses just 54 percent of its SaaS licenses ([Zylo 2026 SaaS Management Index](https://zylo.com/blog/how-much-wasted-on-saas-spend)). License utilization is not the same thing as dollars wasted, but it is the cleanest published measure of how much purchased software sits unused while the feature a firm actually needs stays unbuilt.

Ownership does not make software free. It changes whose problem the roadmap is. Someone on his team asks for a change, and, in his experience, it is often live the same day. Not because he is a genius programmer, by his own account, but because the system is his, so the priority list is his.

## What does owning it actually cost?

He would be the first to say the demos skip this part, so this piece will not. When the firm owns the system, it owns everything a vendor used to absorb: maintenance, security, backups, technical debt, model migrations when the underlying AI changes, and documentation nobody feels like writing. Above all it owns key-person risk. For most of the year, the system lived substantially in his head, which is a fragile place for infrastructure to live.

That ledger is real, and it is part of why his build took a year of nights instead of a quarter. The honest conclusion is not that every firm should build. It is that the tradeoff has changed shape: the construction got cheap, while the responsibilities of ownership did not.

## If this is so valuable, why isn't everyone doing it?

His answer had nothing to do with software. Knowing what is possible, because most people have never watched these systems work. Confidence, because owners do not believe they can start, so they never do. Grit, because building means failing repeatedly, and most people quit there. And hours, because running a firm already takes all of them.

The market data backs the first part directly: among family offices, the top reported barrier to AI adoption is lack of internal expertise, cited by 57 percent ([Citi, 2025](https://www.citigroup.com/global/insights/ai-in-the-family-office)). The rest is his read, and the adoption numbers are at least consistent with it. Access to the models is solved: generative AI reached 58 percent of small businesses in 2025, up from 23 percent two years earlier ([U.S. Chamber of Commerce, 2025](https://www.uschamber.com/technology/empowering-small-business-the-impact-of-technology-on-u-s-small-business)).

Turning access into a system a firm actually runs on is harder, and far fewer firms get there. Everyone can start. Very few finish.

## Why does a different tool for every person matter?

The discovery he called most valuable had nothing to do with raw intelligence. Building a genuinely different version of the system for each person, their own layout, their own workflow, used to be a year of development. He puts it at about an hour now.

He noticed what happened next: when the tool fit the person, they used it, and they told him so. His staff sell the system inside the firm better than he does. He is one data point and he would say so, but his hypothesis deserves to be stated plainly, because it cuts against how software is sold: the adoption lever is fit, not intelligence. The industry still mostly sells roles and seat licenses. Personalization at this depth is not yet the norm, and in his firm it turned out to be the difference between software that gets used and software that gets routed around.

## What's actually the hardest part?

His words, near the end of the conversation: the hardest part is collecting the context. What the firm actually knows, how the work really happens, the questions people do not know they should answer. The building is fast now. Knowing what to build, and feeding it the firm's real knowledge, is the work.

Which is why the memory comes first. Tools built on a firm that has not gathered what it knows are demos. It is also why "just hire a developer" tends to underdeliver here: a coder who does not speak your industry cannot collect your context, and, as he put it, people who know the code usually have no idea what you do every day. One side effect he noticed along the way: a firm whose deals, decisions, and reasons live in one place is simply easier to examine, for a new hire or anyone else who needs to understand it.

## So where is the value?

Not a shinier chat window. The tool, the memory, and the fit.

He had the knowledge, the confidence, and the grit, and it still took him a year of failing forward. What the year taught him fits in two sentences, and they are the ones worth keeping: the building is fast now. Knowing what to build, and what your firm already knows, is the work.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should a lean financial firm build its own AI system?

A few owners can, and he is proof. The honest checklist: deep industry knowledge, real technology fluency, tolerance for months of failed attempts, spare hours, and a plan for the ownership costs above. Firms with all of that should consider building. Everyone else should buy the outcome without staffing the experiment.

### Why not just hire an engineer?

One person who knows software and also speaks the industry is rare, expensive, and a single point of failure. The context problem is the real blocker: the hardest part is extraction, not construction.

### Is custom AI infrastructure secure enough for financial services?

It can be, and isolation is the point when it is done right: your data in its own environment, encrypted, governed by a data processing agreement, with humans approving changes. Ask any provider the questions worth asking before any system touches client data, and get the answers in writing.
