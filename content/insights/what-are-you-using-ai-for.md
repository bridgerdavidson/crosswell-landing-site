On Wall Street Oasis, private equity professionals keep asking each other the same thing, almost sheepishly: ["Are you guys using AI to do your PE work?"](https://www.wallstreetoasis.com/forum/private-equity/are-you-guys-using-ai-to-do-your-pe-work) and ["What are you actually using AI for in PE?"](https://www.wallstreetoasis.com/forum/private-equity/what-are-you-actually-using-ai-for-in-pe) The honest answers cluster fast: transcribing expert calls, first drafts of memos, summarizing CIMs. For a lean financial firm, six workflows hold up in practice: meetings that file themselves, briefings prepared before the meeting, onboarding that starts from the firm's memory, recurring reports that assemble themselves, document intake that reads itself, and the ability to ask the firm what it already knows. Agentic AI, in plain words, is AI that does the work rather than just answering questions. And the six are not really six. They are one loop.

## Why has adoption outrun integration?

Charles Schwab's RIA and AI study found 63 percent of RIAs using AI tools in some capacity, more than double since 2023, while only about one in ten are fully integrating AI into their business strategy ([Schwab, January 2026](https://pressroom.aboutschwab.com/press-releases/press-release/2026/Schwab-Study-Reveals-RIA-AI-Adoption-More-Than-Doubles---But-Most-Firms-Still-in-Early-Stages/default.aspx)). That gap between trying and running on it is the story of this moment, and closing it is less about picking better tools than about how the tools connect.

## Why look at the six as a loop, not a list?

Meetings create memory. Briefings consume it. Onboarding queries it. Reports assemble from it. Intake feeds it. Ask the firm retrieves across all of it.

Bought separately, each is a decent app with its own login and its own silo, and the value of each caps out on its own. Connected, the output of one workflow becomes context for the next: the meeting filed on Tuesday sharpens the briefing on Thursday, the documents read at intake show up in the quarterly report, and everything any of them capture is there when a new hire starts asking questions. That connection, not any single workflow, is what most firms have not built. It is why so many land at "uses AI sometimes" and stall.

## What are the six use cases that actually hold up?

**1. Meetings that file themselves.** The note-taker boom is real: Bob Veres, co-author of the T3/Inside Information survey, says AI note-takers reached a combined 42.86 percent market share among the survey's 2,906 advisory respondents in a single year, and he estimates it is probably over 60 percent now ([T3/Inside Information, 2026](https://t3technologyhub.com/live-from-t3-key-wealthtech-and-ai-findings-from-the-2026-t3-inside-information-software-survey/)). But capture alone is where most firms stall. Notes that land in a field and connect to nothing are a transcript graveyard. The version that compounds links every meeting to the deal it touched, the people in it, and the decision that came out of it. One caution for regulated firms: retention rules likely apply to those notes too.

**2. Briefings before the meeting, not minutes after.** The same memory that files the last conversation should prepare the next one. Walk in holding everything the firm knows about the person across the table: the history, the open items, the thing they said eight months ago. This is the difference between a note-taker and a memory. One writes down what happened. The other makes the next hour better.

**3. Onboarding that starts on day one.** Most new hires spend their first months reconstructing context interview by interview: how we do things, why we passed on that deal, who to ask. When the firm's knowledge lives in one connected place, they inherit it on day one and question it all year. It pays in both directions: the firm ramps people faster, and what people learn stays when they go.

**4. The report that ate a Friday.** Every firm has one. The recurring report assembled by hand from five sources, every week, forever. This is where agentic AI moves from answering to working: it assembles the draft from the firm's memory, a person reviews it, and the Friday comes back. The demand is visible even in the most private corner of the industry: among family offices using AI, investment performance reporting more than doubled in a year, to 16 percent ([Citi Wealth Global Family Office Report, 2025](https://www.citigroup.com/global/insights/ai-in-the-family-office)).

**5. Intake that reads itself.** CIMs, sub docs, statements, K-1s. Documents arrive, get read, and become structured records, with a person approving what lands. The fund professionals on those forum threads already do the first half of this with consumer tools, one PDF at a time. The compounding version writes into the firm's memory instead of into one analyst's chat history. IDC research found 22 percent of enterprise content gets recreated from scratch because nobody can find the existing version ([IDC, 2023](https://blog.box.com/90-your-data-unstructured-and-its-full-untapped-value)). Intake that lands in a findable, shared record is how that number starts falling.

**6. Ask the firm what it knows.** The deals passed on and the reasons why. The judgment paid for the hard way. Once the first five workflows feed a shared memory, this one is less a new tool than a door into what the others have been saving. One discipline makes it trustworthy: insist on answers that show their sources. An answer you cannot trace back to the meeting or the document it came from is a guess with confidence.

## Why does the gap persist?

Buying a note-taker is a purchase. Connecting meetings, email, files, and the CRM into one memory is infrastructure. Most lean firms have nobody whose job that is, which is why nine in ten advisors are still at "uses AI sometimes" instead of "runs on it" ([Schwab, 2026](https://pressroom.aboutschwab.com/press-releases/press-release/2026/Schwab-Study-Reveals-RIA-AI-Adoption-More-Than-Doubles---But-Most-Firms-Still-in-Early-Stages/default.aspx)).

The first use case is rarely found in a brainstorm. It is found by mapping where knowledge already flows through the firm, and where it currently dies: the meeting that never got filed, the analysis sitting in one person's chat history, the report rebuilt from scratch each week.

## How many of the six actually compound?

Count how many of the six your firm already does in some form. Then count how many of those write into something the firm keeps. The first number is adoption. The second is the one that compounds.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are private equity and credit funds actually using AI, or just talking about it?

Using it, mostly quietly and mostly for reading and writing: expert call transcription, memo first drafts, CIM summaries. The gap between firms is not usage. It is whether any of that work accrues to the firm or evaporates in personal chat histories.

### What is the biggest blocker for financial firms?

Compliance and data security, by a wide margin, and the concern is rational when the tools in question are consumer chat apps. The fix is not avoiding the technology. It is running it on infrastructure where the firm controls where data lives.

### Where should a lean firm start?

Not with a tool. With a map: what does the firm know, where does it live, and where does it die today? A focused knowledge audit, a couple of weeks of work, usually surfaces the first use case on its own.
